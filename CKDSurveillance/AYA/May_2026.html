Chronic Kidney Disease in the United States: The New CDC Fact Sheet is Published

May 2026

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) occurs when the kidneys become damaged and over time may not clean the blood as well as healthy kidneys. It is defined by either low kidney function based on a blood test and/or evidence of kidney damage usually detected by testing for the presence of a protein called albumin in the urine, present for at least 3 months1. If kidneys don't work well, toxic waste and extra fluid accumulate in the body. This may lead to high blood pressure, heart disease, stroke, and early death. However, people with CKD and people at risk for CKD can take steps to protect their kidneys with the help of their health care providers.

The CDC published a Fact Sheet in March 20262 highlighting national estimates of chronic kidney disease (see Infographic). Statistics presented were estimated using the August 2021–August 2023 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) data3. Kidney function was expressed as the glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was estimated using CKD-EPI equation without race4. CKD stage 5 (kidney failure) was not included.

The factsheet provides Key Statistics (see infographic) and an overview of the epidemiology of CKD in the United States. It provides statistics on the prevalence of CKD by age groups, race and ethnicity groups, and among individuals with diabetes and high blood pressure.

Having CKD increases the chances of having cardiovascular diseases including stroke. Managing high blood pressure, blood sugar, and cholesterol levels—all factors that increase the risk for heart disease and stroke—is very important for people with CKD. Adults with CKD are at a high risk of dying earlier than adults of similar age without CKD.

The good news is that many options now exist for managing CKD, including medicines to slow the decline in kidney function. These can include monitoring and managing blood sugar and blood pressure; practicing healthy eating in consultation with a dietician, stopping smoking, and getting optimal levels of physical activity to prevent more kidney damage. Avoidance of certain over the counter medications (e.g., ibuprofen) and other toxins (e.g., lead in drinking water) is important.
