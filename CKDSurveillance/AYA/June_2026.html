Chronic Kidney Disease in the United States: The New CDC Report on the State of CKD

June 2026

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) occurs when the kidneys become damaged and over time may not clean the blood as well as healthy kidneys. It is diagnosed based on low kidney function on a blood test and/or high levels of a protein called albumin in the urine for at least 3 months.1 In March 2026, CDC published a report2 with updated national estimates of CKD (see infographic), based on data from the August 2021–August 2023 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES).3

The report provides key numbers and an overview of how common CKD is across age, sex, and race and ethnicity, and among adults with diabetes or high blood pressure.

The good news is that many options now exist for managing CKD, including medicines to slow kidney damage and improve heart health. People with CKD can monitor and manage blood sugar and blood pressure, work with a dietitian to eat healthier foods, stop smoking, and get regular physical activity to help prevent further kidney damage. People with CKD should also avoid certain over-the-counter medications (such as ibuprofen) and toxins that can harm kidneys (such as lead in drinking water).
